**Requirement, as stated (D1):** multiple agents acting toward a shared goal,
with an **externally determined verdict on whether the COLLECTIVE outcome was

files). The requirement evaluated here is the prompt's prose, not a preregistered

## D1. What was searched, and what was found

| candidate | supplies traces? | supplies a COLLECTIVE external verdict? | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public multi-agent LLM trace datasets | no | — | none located |
| **CooperBench** (652 tasks, 12 libraries, 4 languages) | **no — it is an environment** | **yes, mechanically** | the verdict source exists |
| Multi-author GitHub PRs | **yes, harvestable now** | partly — see D1c | structurally wrong shape |
| Agent-evaluation surveys | n/a | n/a | confirm the gap |

### (a) No public dataset of real multi-agent traces with team-level verdicts

Searching for trace corpora with collective success labels returns benchmark
*frameworks* and single-agent trace distillation, not labelled multi-agent
traces. The 2025–26 survey literature states the gap directly: current benchmarks
**lack standardized metrics for credit assignment or group resilience**, which is
precisely the quantity AG-style routes would need. A gap named in a survey is not
proof of absence, but it is the strongest available negative evidence and it
agrees with the search result.

### (b) CooperBench — the verdict property exists, the traces do not

`CooperBench` (ICLR 2026 workshop; 652 tasks across 12 open-source libraries in
Python, TypeScript, Go, Rust) assigns **two agents different features that can be
implemented independently but may conflict without coordination**, and scores a
task successful **only when both agents' patches merge cleanly and both pass
their respective test suites**.

That scoring rule is exactly the required property: it is **mechanical**, it is
**collective** (neither agent can pass alone), and it is **external** — the test
suites belong to the upstream libraries, not to the benchmark author and not to
this program. Its headline is that agent teams score ~30% lower collaborating
than working solo.

**But CooperBench is an environment, not a corpus.** It supplies tasks and a
verdict function; the traces must be generated by running agents in it. So:

- the **verdict** would be genuinely external — the self-authorship trap does
  **not** apply to it;
- the **traces** would be mine, and the cost is real: 652 tasks × 2 agents ×
  multi-turn coding, against **$3.12 remaining**. That is one to two orders of
  magnitude short.

**This is the substantive finding: requirement four is studiable in principle.
The blocker is trace-generation budget, not the absence of a verdict.** That is a
materially different — and more useful — conclusion than "not achievable".

### (c) Multi-author PRs — harvestable, and the wrong shape

Measured on 26 PRs with ≥3 commits across both corpus repositories:

| distinct commit authors | scikit-learn | pydantic |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 14 | 3 |
| 2 | 4 | 2 |
| ≥3 | 2 | 1 |

**9 / 26 (0.346) are multi-author**, and the CI verdict on the head commit is a
genuine collective verdict over all authors' work. Some carry
`Co-authored-by:` trailers (one PR had 12 such commits), so even single commits
are sometimes joint.

**Rejected anyway, for a reason that is not about externality.** The agents here
are **serialized through git**, not concurrent. The dominant pattern is a
maintainer pushing fixups onto a contributor's branch — sequential authorship
under review, with conflicts resolved by rebase *before* CI ever runs. The
interference that makes multi-agent coordination interesting is **removed by the
version-control workflow before the verdict is computed.** Using this population
would produce a number about collaborative *outcomes* while silently containing
no coordination *failures*, because git already ate them. That is the same
species as artifact #17: a plausible quantity that is not the one being asked
about.

## D2. The minimum properties a source must have

Stated as a specification, because this is the part that tells us whether the
requirement is studiable at all.

A usable multi-agent source must have **all six**:

1. **≥2 agents with genuinely concurrent authority** over a shared artifact —
   able to act without seeing each other's completed work. Serialized authorship
   (git PRs) fails here.
2. **A shared goal neither agent can satisfy alone**, so the verdict is not a
   disguised max() over individual outcomes.
3. **A collective verdict computed mechanically**, by executing something —
   not by a judge model and not by a human rating.
4. **The verdict owned by a third party.** Upstream test suites qualify;
   anything authored alongside the trace does not.
5. **Recorded interference**: the trace must retain the coordination attempts
   (messages, conflicting edits, retries), not just the final artifact. A source
   that keeps only outcomes cannot support credit assignment.
6. **Both outcome classes present** without selection on success — the
   requirement that disqualified this repository's own history.

**Does such a source exist? Yes — CooperBench satisfies 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6,
and 5 depends on the harness that runs it.** It is the only candidate located
that satisfies (4) without the traces being self-authored.

**What it would cost to unblock:** generating traces over a usable subset. A
100-task subset at 2 agents × multi-turn coding is the smallest thing that could
support a decomposition-level claim, and it is well beyond the current balance.
The requirement is therefore **BLOCKED ON BUDGET, NOT ON DESIGN** — the same
class of blocker as SR-4, and it should be recorded the same way.
